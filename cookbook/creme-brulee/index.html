Raspberry Crème Brûlée

Vanilla custard with a raspberry kick under a shatter-crisp caramel lid. Six 6-oz ramekins, about 4 oz of custard each. Bake Friday, torch Saturday.

🍮 Serves 6 🔥 Bake 25–30 min at 325°F ❄ Chill 4 hr + 🗓 Split over Friday → Saturday

What's different in this version

  • Raspberry extract in the custard, vanilla alongside it. Half-teaspoon per 720 g cream. Extracts go bitter fast, so the vanilla carries and the raspberry accents. Both go in off heat — heat dulls them.
  • 4 oz per 6-oz ramekin (about two-thirds full). Shallower custard bakes faster, sets more reliably, and gives you more caramel lid per bite.
  • Superfine sugar for the lid, 1 tsp per ramekin. Blitz granulated in a blender for 30 seconds. Superfine melts at lower heat, so the custard underneath stays cold. Never powdered sugar — its cornstarch goes sticky instead of crackly.
  • Make-ahead split: bake and chill Friday, torch Saturday right before serving. The lid softens within an hour of torching, so it's always a day-of job.

2

Get ready — Friday

  1. Heat the oven, stage the pan

    Preheat to 325°F, rack in the middle. Set the 6 ramekins in a roasting pan, spaced so they don't touch. Fill a kettle with about 8 cups (2 L) of water and leave it on the stove — don't boil it yet.

    Why the water: the custards bake sitting in hot water, which shields them from direct oven heat so they set silky instead of curdling. The water surrounds the ramekins; it never touches the custard.

  2. Warm the cream

    Pour 720 g cream into a medium saucepan over medium heat. Ready in about 5 minutes, when steam rises and tiny bubbles ring the edge. Turn off the heat, leave the pan where it is.

    Stop: don't let the cream reach a full boil — boiled cream scorching and forming a skin is how a grainy custard starts.

  3. Blitz your superfine sugar (while the cream warms)

    Pulse about 50 g (¼ cup) granulated sugar in a blender or food processor for 30 seconds — fine but not powdered. Set aside in a small bowl for Saturday. You need 6 teaspoons; the extra is insurance.

    Why bother: superfine melts at a lower temperature, so the lid caramelizes before the cold custard underneath warms up. That's the thin-shell trick.

3

Make the custard

  1. Whisk the yolks

    Whisk 7 yolks (~126 g), 100 g sugar, and ⅛ tsp salt in a medium bowl for about 1 minute, until pale and slightly thick.

    First time separating eggs? Crack one at a time over a small bowl, pass the yolk between shell halves, let the white drop. Cold eggs separate more cleanly. Save the whites — they keep 4 days.

  2. Temper, slowly

    Whisk the yolks nonstop with one hand. Pour the warm cream in a thin, slow stream with the other — take a full 30–60 seconds to add all of it.

    Stop: cream poured in fast cooks the yolks into sweet scrambled eggs. Thin stream, constant whisk.

  3. Extracts, off heat

    Stir in ¾ tsp vanilla and ½ tsp raspberry extract now, after the cream is in — not in the saucepan. Heat dulls both and can make the raspberry taste sharper.

    Stop: resist adding more raspberry to make it stronger. Past ½ tsp per 720 g cream it reads as candy, not berry. Taste the mix; it should smell faintly of raspberry.

  4. Berries in, strain over

    Toss 18 raspberries in a pinch of sugar. Put 3 in the bottom of each ramekin. Pour the custard through a fine-mesh sieve into a spouted pitcher, then fill each ramekin to about two-thirds full — 4 oz / ~120 g each. Skim any surface foam.

    What to expect: a few berries float at first; most settle as they bake. The sugaring keeps their juice from bleeding into the custard.

4

Water-bath bake

  1. Boil, fill, flood — at the oven

    Boil the kettle. Pull the middle rack halfway out, set the pan on it, then pour the boiling water into a corner of the pan until it reaches halfway up the ramekins. Slide the rack in, close the door.

    Stop: never pour water over or into the ramekins — water in the custard ruins the texture. Filling at the oven also means you never carry a full pan of boiling water across the kitchen.

  2. Bake, then jiggle-test

    Bake 25–30 minutes at 325°F — the shallower 4-oz fill runs faster than classic brûlée. At 25 minutes, nudge the pan with a mitted hand: done = edges hold still, only the centers wobble like set Jell-O (about 170°F internal, if you use a thermometer).

    Stop: a center that doesn't move at all is overbaked — that's grainy custard. Start checking at 25 minutes, not 30.

    Whole surface rippling? Bake 5 more minutes, test again.

5

Chill overnight

  1. Cool 30 minutes, then into the fridge

    Pull the whole pan out and set it on the stovetop. Lift each ramekin out of the water — tongs plus a thin spatula, or grip with a folded dish towel — onto a wire rack. Cool 30 minutes, then refrigerate uncovered for at least 4 hours. Overnight is ideal for a Saturday service.

    Keeping them past a day? Cover with plastic wrap only once they're fully cold — covering warm custard traps drippy condensation. They hold 2 days in the fridge, which is exactly your Friday-to-Saturday window.

6

Brûlée day — Saturday

  1. Blot, sugar thin, dump the excess

    Blot the custard surface dry with the corner of a paper towel — sugar won't caramelize evenly on a wet surface. Sprinkle 1 tsp superfine sugar over each custard — no more. Tilt and rotate to coat edge to edge, then tap out the excess.

    Stop: too much sugar is the number-one cause of thick, patchy shells. A thin shell cracks clean; a thick one piles up and goes bitter at the edges.

  2. Torch to copper amber

    Hold the flame tip 2–3 inches above the sugar and move in slow, steady sweeps. Sugar goes wet → bubbling → amber. Stop at copper penny amber, one shade lighter than you want — it keeps darkening for a few seconds after the flame moves on.

    Stop: keep the flame moving the entire time. A flame parked in one spot turns that patch black and bitter before the rest even melts.

  3. Wait, garnish, crack

    Wait 1–2 minutes. Dab a fresh raspberry dry with a paper towel and set one on each custard — after torching, so the juice never weeps onto the crack. The caramel is ready when the back of a spoon taps against it like glass. Tap, listen, serve.

    No torch? Freeze the custards 15 minutes first so the broiler doesn't warm them through, then broil on the top rack 2–4 minutes with the door cracked open. Watch the entire time — it turns fast.

7

Storage

Chilled custard

2 days in the fridge, uncovered until fully cold, then covered. The caramel lid starts softening after about an hour — brûlée only right before serving, never in advance.

Seven lonely egg whites

They keep 4 days in the fridge. Meringues, financiers, or a Saturday-morning egg-white omelet all count as responsible ownership.

If something went wrong

Thick, patchy shell

Too much sugar went on. Next time 1 tsp per ramekin, tilt to coat, dump the excess off before torching.

Lid went sticky, not snappy

Powdered sugar or a torched-ahead lid. Superfine sugar only, and torch within the hour before serving.

Black bitter patches

The flame parked in one spot. Keep it sweeping, 2–3 inches above, and stop at copper amber — carryover heat does the last shade.

Raspberry tastes like candy

Too much extract. Cap it at ½ tsp per 720 g cream and let the vanilla do the rounding. Next batch, hold it at ⅜ tsp if your extract is strong.

Grainy custard

Overbaked. With the shallower 4-oz fill, start the jiggle test at 25 minutes and pull while the centers still wobble.

Sweet scrambled yolks in the bowl

The hot cream went in too fast. Pour in a thin stream over a full 30–60 seconds, whisking nonstop.
